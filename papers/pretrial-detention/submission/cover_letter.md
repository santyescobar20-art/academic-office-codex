Dear Editors,

I submit for your consideration the manuscript "Can a Court Curb Pretrial Detention? Evidence from a Binding Supreme Court Resolution in Ecuador".

Apex courts in Latin America often try to limit pretrial detention by tightening the reasons judges must give for ordering it. The article tests whether one such rule worked. In December 2021 Ecuador's National Court of Justice issued a generally binding resolution requiring prosecutors to prove, and judges to explain, the necessity of detention. I link every committal order issued by Ecuadorian criminal courts between 2016 and 2026, at judge level, to hearings, homicides and weekly prison counts.

Detention fell by a fifth the following year, and a before-and-after comparison would credit the Court. The evidence points elsewhere. The decline started before the resolution, was no larger where detention had been most routine, left no break in the weekly prison series at the relevant date, and was fully reversed by 2025 with the rule still in force. The paper reports this null result with bounds on the effects it can exclude.

I believe the manuscript fits the Journal for three reasons. It evaluates a legal rule with administrative data and a transparent design; it extends an empirical literature on pretrial detention that is almost wholly about the United States; and it addresses a question of general interest, namely whether a court can change the behaviour of its own lower courts by declaration.

The manuscript is original, is not under review elsewhere, and has not been published in whole or in part. All data are public and a full replication package accompanies the submission.
